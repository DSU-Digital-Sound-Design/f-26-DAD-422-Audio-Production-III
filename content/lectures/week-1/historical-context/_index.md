# Historical context

### How cinema got from one channel to hundreds

---

<h2 class="r-fit-text">Every format was a bet.</h2>

{{% note %}}
The point of this history: every format we use this semester was once a gamble a studio made on new hardware. Watching the channel count grow from mono to Atmos is also watching what filmmakers wanted audiences to feel, and what exhibitors were willing to pay for.

# Before sound was recorded with the picture

---

### The Assassination of the Duke of Guise (1908)

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/IyfO06O8nTQ" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen></iframe>

Music by Camille Saint-Saëns, written scene by scene

{{% note %}}
Often cited as the first film with an **original score composed for that specific film**. *La Mort du duc de Guise* premiered in Paris on 16 November 1908, and Saint-Saëns later adapted the music into concert form as Op. 128.

Worth naming for students: the score was written scene by scene, but it was played live in the room. Nothing was recorded with the picture yet. That gap is the whole problem the next thirty years try to solve.

# Mono optical sound

## 1927 to the mid 1970s

**One channel.** Not necessarily one speaker.

**1927.** *The Jazz Singer* takes sync sound commercial.

**Fifty years.** Nothing displaced it until the mid 1970s.

{{% note %}}
With synchronized sound around 1927, film moved to **mono optical soundtracks**. That arrangement held for roughly fifty years, which is far longer than students expect.

**Make the channels-versus-speakers distinction here, it pays off all hour.** A mono house was not necessarily a one-speaker house. Standard practice was one or more *bridged* loudspeakers behind a sound-permeable screen. J. P. Maxfield of Bell Labs describes the typical theater as having a single horn placed centrally to anchor dialogue to the picture. But larger houses ran a **bank of three or five speakers** behind the screen to fill the room, and every one of them reproduced the **same single channel**. The point was that all screen audio should seem to come from the screen.

So the thing that changes at Fantasound and then CinemaScope is not the number of speakers. Three screen speakers already existed. What is new is three speakers carrying three *different* signals. Channels, not speakers.

Verified against the Immersive Audio notebook (Beck, *A Quiet Revolution*, citing Maxfield), 2026-08-29.

*The Jazz Singer* (1927) is the usual marker for the first commercially successful sync-sound feature.
{{%/ note %}}

### King Kong (1933)

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/MMNICLfHE3M" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen></iframe>

Separate tracks for dialogue, effects and music

{{% note %}}
Max Steiner's score for *King Kong* is widely regarded as the first **feature-length original score** for an American sound film. It brought in thematic orchestral scoring with leitmotifs tied to characters, and recording on **separate tracks** for dialogue, effects and music.

The separate-tracks part is the one that matters for this course. Once those three elements live on their own tracks, someone has to decide how they balance, and the job of mixing exists.

Sources: [Filmsite.org](https://www.filmsite.org/kingk.html), [David Allen Composer](https://davidallencomposer.com/blog/king-kong-max-steiner-james-newton-howard-comparison)
{{%/ note %}}

# Fantasound (1940)

## The first multichannel system

---

## Fantasia and Fantasound

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/2FRyAXXOsMo" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen></iframe>

**3 channels + 1 control track.** Three speakers behind the screen.

**96 speakers.** The surround array at the Los Angeles premiere.

**Six theaters.** All it was ever installed in.

{{% note %}}
Walt Disney's *Fantasia* introduced **Fantasound**, the first commercial multichannel system in a cinema.

The configuration: a dual-projection system running **three discrete optical audio channels plus one control track** on a separate synchronized strip of film. The three audio channels fed three screen speakers behind the screen. The control track governed volume and automated the panning of sound out to the surround array. For the Los Angeles premiere that array was **ninety-six smaller speakers** placed around and behind the audience.

Verified against the Immersive Audio NotebookLM notebook (Beck, *A Quiet Revolution*; Ouzounian, *Stereophonica*; Théberge/Devine/Everrett, *Living Stereo*), 2026-08-29. An earlier version of this slide claimed 64 speakers split 36 screen / 22 orchestra / 6 balcony; the notebook sources do not support that breakdown.

Two aesthetics were in play from day one, and they are still the two options today:
- **Correct**: realistic orchestral placement, the orchestra sounds like it is where it would be.
- **Spectacular**: sound moves and swirls for effect.

**The real first here:** this is the first time three screen speakers carried three *different* recorded channels rather than one channel bridged across several speakers. Compare back to the mono slide.

It was never economically sustainable. The notebook sources describe Fantasound as a one-shot trial installed in six theaters nationwide before it disappeared. You will also see "about a dozen" cited elsewhere; the counts differ depending on whether you mean installations or roadshow engagements.

Background: [Fantasound (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fantasound), [New Yorker, 1940](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1940/11/16/fantasound)
{{%/ note %}}

# The Dolby Stereo era

## Mid 1970s to mid 1990s

---

### Dolby Stereo arrives in two steps

{{< slide-columns >}}
{{< slide-column title="Lisztomania (1975)" >}}
**Three channels: L, C, R**

The derived center channel stabilized screen audio and killed the "hole in the middle."
{{< /slide-column >}}
{{< slide-column title="A Star Is Born (1976)" >}}
**Four channels: L, C, R, S**

The matrixed surround channel was added at the request of director Frank Pierson and producer Jon Peters, who wanted live-concert ambience from the back of the house.
{{< /slide-column >}}
{{< /slide-columns >}}

{{% note %}}
This two-step distinction is the correction worth making out loud. Dolby Stereo did not arrive fully formed as LCRS.

*Lisztomania* (1975) was three-channel LCR. The full four-channel LCRS configuration did not arrive until *A Star Is Born* (1976), and the surround channel was added because two specific people wanted a specific effect, not because the engineers planned it.

Verified against the Immersive Audio notebook (Beck, *A Quiet Revolution: Changes in American Film Sound Practices, 1967-1979*), 2026-08-29.

The trick underneath all of it is **matrixing**: four channels folded into two optical tracks using phase and amplitude, so a mono or stereo house could still play the same print. We cover matrixing properly in week 6.

Background: [Dolby Stereo history](https://everything.explained.today/Dolby_Stereo/), [Dolby, A Century of Innovation](https://professional.dolby.com/siteassets/cinema/cie-innovation-tech/cie-innovation-tech-dolby.pdf)
{{%/ note %}}

The film that made exhibitors install the format

{{% note %}}
*Star Wars* (1977) is the breakthrough moment for Dolby Stereo. Spatialized effects plus John Williams' score reset what audiences expected a blockbuster to sound like, and gave exhibitors a commercial reason to install the format.

### Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977)

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/dSpQ3G08k48" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen></iframe>

**First film to use dedicated subwoofers in theaters**

{{% note %}}
Correction from the earlier version of this deck: this is a **1977** film, not 1978. It had its 70 mm showcase release in November 1977, followed by a wider national release in early 1978, which is probably where the 1978 date came from.

It was exhibited two ways:
- **70 mm six-track magnetic** with Dolby A noise reduction, carrying a dedicated low-frequency "baby boom" track. This made it the **first film to use dedicated subwoofers in theaters**.
- **35 mm stereo optical** nationally, using matrixed four-channel Dolby Stereo.

That baby boom track is the ancestor of the LFE channel in every 5.1 mix you will make this semester.

Verified against the Immersive Audio notebook (Beck, *A Quiet Revolution*), 2026-08-29.

Background: [AFI catalog](https://catalog.afi.com/Film/67160-CLOSE-ENCOUNTERSOFTHETHIRDKIND)

# The digital surround era

## Early 1990s to now

---

### 1987: the meeting where 5.1 got its name

> People were saying, "How many channels should there be?" And people said two, people said four, one said eight. And I put my hand up and said, "five point one." Everybody went, "What is he talking about?"
>
> -- <cite>Tomlinson Holman</cite>

{{% note %}}
At the October 1987 SMPTE meeting a subcommittee met to set a digital cinema sound standard and argued over how many discrete channels it should require. Tomlinson Holman proposed "five point one." After he explained what a tenth of a channel meant, a frequency-limited LFE channel for low-end headroom, 5.1 was agreed as the minimum standard.

Three front channels (L, C, R), two surrounds (Ls, Rs), and LFE. All discrete, not matrixed. That is the layout the whole industry then built toward.

Verified against the Immersive Audio notebook (Holman, *Surround Sound: Up and Running*; Kerins, *Beyond Dolby (Stereo)*), 2026-08-29. Holman's own book is where this quote comes from, and the week 6 deck uses the fuller version.

Ask: why would a committee pick a number that no hardware yet supported?
{{%/ note %}}

### Three formats, eighteen months

{{< slide-columns >}}
{{< slide-column title="Dolby Digital (1992)" >}}
*Batman Returns*, 19 June 1992

Originally called Dolby SR-D. Opened in **ten** equipped theaters.

5.1 discrete, data printed between the sprocket holes.
{{< /slide-column >}}
{{< slide-column title="DTS (1993)" >}}
*Jurassic Park*, June 1993

Opened in over **a thousand** DTS-equipped theaters.

5.1 on external CD-ROMs, synced to the print by timecode.
{{< /slide-column >}}
{{< /slide-columns >}}

{{% note %}}
*Batman Returns* was the first feature released in Dolby Digital, on 19 June 1992, in a limited run of ten equipped theaters.

DTS debuted with *Jurassic Park* in June 1993, backed by Spielberg and Universal, and opened in over a thousand DTS-equipped theaters. Note the difference in scale: Dolby went first, DTS went wide.

Verified against the Immersive Audio notebook (Kerins, *Beyond Dolby (Stereo)*), 2026-08-29.

The formats differed in where the data physically lived, which is why a single print could carry all three plus the analog track.

Background: [Dolby news](https://news.dolby.com/en-WW/252716-the-bat-is-back-in-dolby-as-tim-burton-s-batman-and-batman-returns-swoop-back-to-theaters-exclusively-at-dolby-cinema/), [The Digital Bits](https://thedigitalbits.com/columns/michael-coate/history-legacy-showmanship/item/1611-batman-returns-25th)

### Sony Dynamic Digital Sound (SDDS)

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/ShBw43KJoLk" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen></iframe>

*Last Action Hero* (1993), one week after DTS

{{% note %}}
SDDS premiered with *Last Action Hero* in 1993, roughly **one week after** DTS debuted with *Jurassic Park*. Three competing digital formats inside eighteen months.

- Up to 8 channels: five screen (L, Lc, C, Rc, R) plus stereo surrounds and LFE.
- Most mixes were still delivered as 5.1.
- Limited long-term adoption; cost and complexity killed it.

Verified against the Immersive Audio notebook (Kerins, *Beyond Dolby (Stereo)*), 2026-08-29.

The five-across screen array is worth pausing on. Sony bet that wider screens needed more front channels. Atmos effectively revives that idea two decades later.
{{%/ note %}}

### Adding channels behind you

{{< slide-columns >}}
{{< slide-column title="6.1 — Dolby Surround EX" >}}
*The Phantom Menace*, 1999

A **matrixed** rear center, steered out of the existing surround pair.
{{< /slide-column >}}
{{< slide-column title="7.1 — Dolby Surround 7.1" >}}
*Toy Story 3*, 2010

A **discrete** rear pair, splitting the surrounds into four zones.
{{< /slide-column >}}
{{< /slide-columns >}}

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/bD7bpG-zDJQ" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen></iframe>

{{% note %}}
Dolby Surround EX added a sixth channel by **matrixing** a center surround out of the existing Ls and Rs pair. First used on *Star Wars Episode I: The Phantom Menace* (1999). Note that matrixing comes back here, twenty-four years after Dolby Stereo used the same trick.

Dolby Surround 7.1 (2010, first with *Toy Story 3*) split the surrounds into four discrete zones instead.

Background: [Dolby Surround 7.1 launch](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20100615006577/en/Disney%E2%80%A2Pixar%E2%80%99s-Toy-Story-3-Makes-Global-Debut-with-Dolby-Surround-7.1), [Dolby Digital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolby_Digital)

# Dolby Atmos (2012)

## Channels become objects

---

## The shift to objects

<iframe width="560" height="315" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/IOXY5VubOr4" title="YouTube video player" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture" allowfullscreen></iframe>

**128 objects, up to 64 speaker feeds.**

**June 2012.** *Brave*, at the El Capitan Theatre in Los Angeles.

{{% note %}}
Atmos is the break in the pattern. Every format before it asked "how many channels?" Atmos asks "where is this sound in the room?" and lets the playback system work out which speakers to use.

The first Dolby Atmos installation was at the El Capitan Theatre in Los Angeles for the premiere of *Brave* in June 2012.

Say the practical consequence plainly: you no longer mix *to a speaker layout*. You place objects, and the renderer adapts to whatever room the film plays in. That is why the same Atmos mix works in a 60-speaker cinema and in headphones.

Source: [Dolby Atmos (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolby_Atmos), checked 2026-08-29.
{{%/ note %}}

### Home audio migration

- **DVD (1996+)** - Dolby Digital and DTS; most Hollywood titles favored Dolby Digital
- **Blu-ray (2006+)** - lossless arrives: Dolby TrueHD, DTS-HD Master Audio, LPCM
- **Streaming** - Atmos delivered over Dolby Digital Plus with Joint Object Coding, for bandwidth

{{% note %}}
The cinema formats are only half the story. Each one had to survive the trip into the living room, and the living room eventually started driving the decisions.

Blu-ray is where Atmos and DTS:X ride as extensions on top of TrueHD and DTS-HD MA.

Background: [DVD-Video](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD-Video), [Dolby TrueHD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolby_TrueHD), [DTS-HD MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DTS-HD_Master_Audio), [Atmos over DD+ JOC](https://professionalsupport.dolby.com/s/article/What-is-Dolby-Digital-Plus-JOC-Joint-Object-Coding)
